Nobody ever typed the answer into a neural network, and that is the strangest thing about them.
Take a classic network that reads handwritten digits, with two hidden layers of sixteen neurons each.
It has thirteen thousand and two weights and biases that can be adjusted.
No human sets those by hand, and at the start they are random, so a seven produces garbage.
So how does random turn into skilled?
My claim: the network never learns an answer, it gets nudged slightly less wrong, millions of times.
Learning just means changing the weights and biases to minimize a cost function.
It happens through one loop with three parts: error, blame, and a small step.
Start with error, because a network can only improve at something it can measure.
The network outputs ten activations, one for each digit, and we know the answer we wanted.
Take the gap between each output and its desired value, and square it.
Then add up all those squares.
For example, a guess of zero point one where the answer is one point zero gives a squared error of zero point eight one.
Do this for every training example and take the average.
That average is the cost, one number saying how wrong the whole network is.
Millions of messy mistakes get squeezed into a single score.
Learning then becomes a clear job: lower that score.
The method is gradient descent, an iterative way of lowering a loss function.
You compute the gradient, then move the weights a small step in the opposite direction.
Google's machine learning course gives the same loop: calculate the loss, find the direction, move a small amount, and repeat.
You stop when the loss can no longer drop.
But thirteen thousand weights all affect that one number differently.
Coming up: how the network works out which weights deserve the blame.
So the cost is high. Which weights caused it?
The answer is an algorithm called backpropagation.
It computes the negative gradient of the cost, the direction that lowers the cost most efficiently.
Think of it as handing out blame, weight by weight.
A weight that multiplies a strongly active neuron has a large influence on the output.
So the best adjustment is in proportion to those activations.
Weights attached to quiet neurons barely change.
But how do you do this for thousands of weights without going mad?
Backpropagation works backward from the output, applying the chain rule again and again.
Each piece computes only its own local effect and needs nothing else.
The Stanford course notes call this a beautifully local process.
Pieces pass a gradient signal back, saying whether they want their outputs pushed up or down.
Each layer's result is computed once and reused for earlier layers, not recomputed for every weight.
The error flows backward, and every weight gets its share of the blame.
Now every weight knows which way to move, but not how far.
The gradient gives a direction, never a distance.
Picture the cost as terrain, and you as a hiker with a blindfold on, feeling the slope and stepping downhill.
If your step is too low, progress is steady but slow.
If it is too high, you go faster but it is riskier, and you can leap over the valley.
Stanford's notes call step size one of the most important, and most headache-inducing, settings in training.
So each step stays small, and you take a great many.
Checking every example before each step would take forever.
Instead the network looks at a small batch of data and steps from that.
It is a noisy estimate, but on average it points the right way, because the errors cancel out.
Three Blue One Brown compares it to a drunk man stumbling downhill, but taking quick steps.
The path zigzags, yet it still heads down.
When more steps stop reducing the loss, the model has converged.
That is learning: not one brilliant leap, but millions of small nudges.
So here is the whole loop.
Measure the error, assign blame to each weight, take a small step, and repeat.
Backpropagation is what makes gradient descent feasible for networks with many layers.
The network never memorized an answer, it just got a little less wrong each time.
But stack many layers, and the blame signal can shrink as it travels backward.
Those vanishing gradients make layers train very slowly, or not at all.
That problem is worth understanding next.
