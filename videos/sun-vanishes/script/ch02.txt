Why would the sky stay bright for eight minutes after the Sun is gone?
Because sunlight is not instant, it travels, and travelling takes time.
The Sun is about one hundred and fifty million kilometers from Earth.
That is about ninety-three million miles, a distance that is hard to picture.
Light covers that gap in about eight point three minutes.
So the sunlight hitting your face right now left the Sun about eight minutes ago.
When the Sun vanishes, that sunlight is already in flight, like a long stream of glowing dots.
Those last rays keep arriving, so the sky looks perfectly normal until the delay is over.
For example, imagine a bullet train at one hundred kilometers an hour heading for the Sun.
It would need roughly one hundred and seventy years to get there.
Light makes the same trip in less time than a short coffee break.
So nothing about the Sun's disappearance can reach us faster than light can carry the news.
But light is only half the story: what about the Sun's pull, the gravity that holds Earth in orbit?
